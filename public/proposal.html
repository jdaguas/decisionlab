DecisionLab

Overthink it, but make it fun.

Some decisions are too complicated for a coin flip but too small for a spreadsheet. DecisionLab lets you describe a dilemma once, then look at it through several lenses: pros & cons, a weighted comparison, 2×2 matrices, and a decision tree. You can also send it to friends for peer review. It helps you see your tradeoffs. It doesn't claim to know the right answer.

The problem

People make many decisions that are too complicated for a coin flip but not important enough to build a spreadsheet for. Should I go to the concert on Wednesday even though my project is due Friday? Which apartment should I take? Should I adopt a dog?

Today the reasoning is scattered. A pros-and-cons list lives in a notes app, the "what do you think?" happens in a group chat, and a poll lives in yet another app. Formal tools feel like homework. Casual ones are too shallow to show the real tradeoff. And nothing puts your own reasoning next to what your friends think, so you can't see where they agree or disagree.

Who it's for

  • Students weighing schoolwork against social plans.
  • Friends settling everyday disagreements or deciding what to do together.
  • Anyone comparing real choices, such as apartments, purchases, events, or job offers, who wants structure without taking it too seriously.

There are two kinds of user. The decider owns the dilemma and does the thinking. The reviewers are friends who open a link on their phone, respond in under a minute, and never have to sign up.

The moment: you've been going back and forth for a day, you've texted three friends, and you still can't tell whether you want to go or just feel like you should.

What I still need to learn

I haven't run user interviews for this project yet, so these are hypotheses, not findings. They're the questions my first round of tests is designed to answer.

  • Is structure too much work for casual decisions? Or does it help, as long as you only have to describe the dilemma once?
  • Is peer review fun enough that friends actually respond and explain why?
  • Do people switch between tools, or settle on one favorite?
  • Do people come back for a second dilemma, or is it a one-time novelty?
  • Does seeing disagreement help? For example: "Your peers say GO; your weighted comparison says STAY."

How it works

  1. Describe it onceThe landing page asks three short questions: what you're deciding, your options (optional, since sometimes you're just gathering opinions), and any dealbreakers (also optional). Paste a Zillow or restaurant link as an option, and it's named for you.
  2. Think it through, with any toolEvery tool works from the same options and dealbreakers, so you never re-enter anything. Mark an option as breaking a dealbreaker once, and it's ruled out everywhere.
  3. Ask others with peer reviewShare a link. With options, friends vote and leave a comment. Without options, they share opinions, and you can turn a good suggestion into an option with one click. If you change your options later, the link updates automatically.
  4. Read the findingsA playful, clearly nonbinding summary of whether your own analysis and your peers agree, written like a lab report: "Replicated!", "Inconclusive", "Conflicting results".
  5. Draw your conclusionSee where each method landed, record your choice, and leave a note for future you.

Ground rules

  • You decide. DecisionLab never presents a score as the "correct" answer. Every playful verdict is labeled nonbinding.
  • Private by default. Your pros, cons, ratings, and trees stay in your browser. Only the question, the options, and (if you allow it) your dealbreakers are shared with reviewers.
  • No advice. No financial, medical, or legal advice, and no predictions of real-world outcomes.

My focus: Design

The biggest risk for DecisionLab is a design risk: does structured thinking feel like homework when the decision is casual? If it does, no amount of backend work will save it. These methods aren't new. A weighted decision matrix comes straight from decision analysis, and Benjamin Franklin described weighing pros against cons as "moral algebra" in 1772. What I'm designing is the experience of using several of them together, quickly.

I want to get better at making structured thinking feel light. Concretely:

1. Describe once, reuse everywhere · removing redundant work
Options and dealbreakers live in one place, and every tool reads from them. Nothing is pre-filled. Suggestions are tap-to-add chips, so you never have to delete something you didn't ask for.
2. One visual language · calm, lab-themed, not cartoonish
A beaker mark, flat cards, and lab vocabulary throughout: peer review, findings, "replicated", "inconclusive". The tone is playful, but the interface stays out of the way.
3. Show disagreement, don't hide it · making tradeoffs visible
The conclusion puts every method side by side. When your methods disagree, the app says so and asks why, instead of averaging the disagreement away.
4. Fix what confuses people · design from observed problems
My own early testing already found a real problem. In the weighted table, option names like "Yes" and "No" read as answers to the "Dealbreaker?" column, and it wasn't clear which way "good" pointed for Cost. Watching new users is how I'll find the rest.

Roadmap

Nov 10 – Nov 17 · v1 shipped

v1: works end to end

  • A guided "describe it once" flow, with optional options (including links) and optional dealbreakers.
  • Five tools on one shared dilemma: Pros & Cons, Weighted, multiple 2×2s, a nested Decision Tree, and Peer Review.
  • Peer review links: no sign-up, open-opinion mode, one-click "add as option", live updates.
  • Findings and a conclusion screen comparing every method.
  • Deployed on Vercel with Supabase, with row-level security on.

Riskiest partSharing across people needs a real backend, so the peer review API was tested end to end (create, vote, re-vote, bad input) before deploying, then again on the live site with real Supabase data.

Nov 18 – Nov 24

Test with real people

  • Give 3 to 5 people a real dilemma of their own. Watch them describe it, try one tool, and send a peer review link, without helping.
  • Watch the reviewers too: can a friend respond from their phone without asking what to do?
  • Write down where people hesitate, which tool they pick, and whether the findings make sense to them.
Nov 25 – Dec 1 · Project fair

v2

This is a draft, and I'll reorder it based on what testing shows.

  • Clearer ratings: a per-criterion hint (e.g. "5 = cheapest") so it's obvious which way is good.
  • A QR code for peer review, for the fair demo: visitors scan, vote, and watch the findings change live.
  • Close a peer review when you're done, and see a history of decisions and how they turned out.
  • A more compact decision tree on phones.
  • If time allows: optional accounts, so dilemmas follow you across devices.

Out of scope

  • AI-generated decisions, or any objective "correct choice" score.
  • Predicting real-world outcomes.
  • Financial, medical, or legal advice.
  • Sophisticated group-consensus algorithms.
  • Native iOS and Android apps.

How I'll know it works

  • A new person can describe a real dilemma, use one tool, and send a peer review link without asking me anything.
  • A friend can open the link on their phone and respond in under a minute.
  • Testers can explain in their own words why the findings say what they say, and at least one says they'd use it again.

Tech stack, MCP servers & CLIs

PieceChoiceWhy it fits
App Next.js, React, TypeScript Interactive tools in the browser, and small server routes for peer review, in one codebase.
Styling Tailwind CSS A small, consistent set of cards, buttons, and colors for the whole lab look.
Your dilemmas Browser storage No login needed for v1, and your private reasoning never leaves your device.
Peer review Supabase (Postgres) Stores shared questions, options, and responses. Row-level security is on with no public access. Only the app's server, using a secret key, can read or write.
Sharing Unguessable links Random 128-bit links for reviewers. A private key for the creator is the only way to edit a live peer review, and only a scrambled (hashed) copy of it is stored.
Hosting Vercel Deploys automatically from GitHub on every push, and keeps keys in environment variables.

MCP servers

  • Supabase MCP: lets Claude Code apply database migrations and check Supabase's security advisors while I build.

CLIs

  • Git & GitHub CLI: version control and the repository.
  • Vercel CLI: deploys, environment variables, and runtime logs for debugging.
  • Node.js / npm: running and building the app.
  • Playwright: scripted browser walkthroughs of each flow, as a new user, before shipping.

Costs & limits

  • Vercel and Supabase stay on their free tiers.
  • There's no paid API. DecisionLab doesn't call an AI model, so there's no model bill.
  • Creating, editing, and voting on peer reviews are rate-limited per network, and every input is checked on the server.
  • Option links must be normal web links (http or https), checked on the server and again before display.
